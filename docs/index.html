          Fuzzy inference system (Mamdani)

          A second AI technique (Lecture 8) on top of the decision tree. The tree classifies the day using yes/no inputs. The fuzzy system reasons with the live numbers (rain mm, PM2.5, day demand), so the advice changes smoothly instead of jumping at a threshold.

          Live data rules

          • Weather: the Open-Meteo hourly forecast for UTP (4.385° N, 100.979° E), up to 16 days ahead. The page fetches it again every 10 minutes, and the prediction uses the weather at the hour you pick on the time slider.
          • Heavy rain: at least 1 mm of rain in that hour, or a rain chance of at least 75%.
          • Haze: PM2.5 of at least 35 µg/m³ in that hour (Malaysian 24-hour standard), from the Open-Meteo air-quality forecast (about 5 days ahead).
          • Meal periods: breakfast 7:00–10:30, lunch 11:00–15:00, tea 15:00–17:00, dinner 17:00–21:00, supper 21:00–23:00. The survey covered lunch and dinner. Other periods use dinner-style rules and are labelled as not surveyed.
          • Calendar: lecture weeks, study break, exams, semester breaks, orientation, blended-learning and "no physical class on campus" weeks, and the holidays printed in the UTP calendar come from docs/data/academic_calendar.js. Perak public holidays (federal + state, including replacement days) are also pulled automatically into docs/data/public_holidays.js by src/update_holidays.py, which a GitHub Action reruns every week; a date in either list counts as a holiday. Orientation and no-class weeks count as Holiday / break, and study break counts as Exam week.
          • Beyond the forecast range, or if you're offline, the weather falls back to Clear. You can override any live value by hand.

          Assumptions (editable)

          • Likert answers are scored 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 (very unlikely → very likely).
          • The normal-weekday baseline is days eaten at the cafeteria per week (capped at 5) ÷ 5 = 0.72.
          • Factors are multiplied together, and demand never goes below 20%.
          • Risk tiers: Low ≥ 80% · Medium ≥ 55% · High below.

          Limitations & ethics

          • Small, skewed sample: 56 students, mostly Year 2 and Village 5 residents.
          • Stated, not observed, behaviour: students reported what they would do.
          • No sales data: the training data is simulated from survey weights.
          • Independence assumption: combined conditions may be over- or under-estimated.
          • Forecasts can be wrong: rain timing in particular is uncertain, so treat the advice as a guide.
          • Ethics: the survey was anonymous and voluntary, with no identifiable data. All outputs are estimates, and the vendor decides.
          • Next step: log real cooked, sold and leftover counts with 1–2 vendors for 2–4 weeks, then retrain on that data.

          How to use the Batch Advisor

          The dashboard answers one question for a cafeteria vendor: "How many portions should I cook for this meal, so I don't waste food and energy?" It looks at the day, the UTP academic calendar and the live weather, predicts how many students will turn up, and turns that into a batch size and a cooking plan.

          1. Pick a date in the week strip on the left (or open the 📅 month calendar).
          2. Drag the time slider to the meal you are planning (lunch, dinner…).
          3. Enter your usual batch: how many portions you cook on a normal day.
          4. Read the risk card: Low cook as usual · Medium cook a bit less · High cook a lot less.
          5. Follow the cooking plan and check the savings tiles.

          Tip: every card has a small ? button that opens its section of this guide.

          Top bar & tabs

          What you see

          • Live weather · Open-Meteo — green dot = forecast loaded. Amber = no haze data, red = offline.

          Daily advisor, part by part

          1 Plan a service (left panel)

          What it tells you

          The situation you are planning for. The day, academic calendar and weather are detected automatically from the live forecast, the UTP calendar and the Perak public-holiday list; you only choose the date, time and batch size.

          How to use it

          • Detected automatically — the day of the week, the calendar period (e.g. Lecture week 11, Final examination, a public holiday) and the forecast for the chosen hour, each with its effect on student footfall.
          • What-if overrides (folded away) — set the calendar (Normal / Exam / Holiday) or weather (Clear / Heavy rain / Haze) by hand to test a scenario. A warning appears while an override is on; ↺ Back to live undoes it.
          • Live data switch — OFF turns the whole panel into manual what-if mode.
          • Short class breaks — switch on if most classes have < 1-hour breaks (students skip lunch more).
          • Usual batch — your normal-day portions. Everything is scaled from this number.

          2 Date picker

          What it tells you

          Which week you are in and what kind of week it is in the UTP calendar (e.g. Lecture week 11, Study break → Final examination, Semester break).

          How to read it

          • ‹ › move one week back or forward. ↺ Today jumps back to today.
          • Each day button shows the date, a weather icon for the chosen time, and a coloured line: lectures study / exams break / no class public holiday.
          • Click the week title to open the month calendar: one row per week, labelled with the lecture week (W8, W9…) or "Study", "Exam", "Break".

          3 Time of service

          What it tells you

          The exact time you are planning for, which meal period it belongs to, and the hourly weather across the day. The prediction changes hour by hour with the forecast.

          How to read it

          • Big time + coloured chip — e.g. 1:00 PM · 🍛 Lunch 11 AM – 3 PM.
          • Coloured bands — the meal periods; click one to jump there. Icons above the slider = forecast for each hour.
          • Updated x min ago — the forecast reloads every 10 minutes; ↻ Refresh does it now, ● Now jumps to the current time.
          • Busyness line — how many lunch customers arrive in this half-hour (from survey Q5).
          • Chart — blue bars = rain each hour; line = expected customers (% of normal), dots coloured by risk.

          Example: dragging from 11 AM (dry) to 1 PM (storm) makes the line drop and the risk turn red.

          4 Live calculation (the animated machine)

          What it tells you

          The whole calculation, step by step, as a machine: inputs → AI engines → answer. It moves whenever anything changes.

          How to read it

          • Four dials — rain (mm/h), air quality (PM2.5), day demand (% of a normal day) and time. Coloured arcs = fuzzy zones; the label underneath names the zone the needle is in.
          • Pipes — dots flow down and speed up when data changes.
          • 🌳 Decision tree — the yes/no questions it asked, lighting up in order, ending in a risk badge.
          • 〰️ Fuzzy logic — the blended output shape; the red line (centroid) is the final score.
          • Formula — usual batch × expected demand = portions to cook, then food and energy saved.
          • Pot — fills to the recommended amount; the dashed line is your usual batch.
          • ▶ Auto-play the day — sweeps 7 AM → 11 PM (1 hour per second at 1×).

          5 Weather & academic calendar cards

          What it tells you

          The live facts behind the prediction for the selected hour and date.

          How to read it

          • Weather — e.g. Thunderstorm expected · 3.6 mm rain · 100% chance · 27°C · PM2.5 6.
          • Academic calendar — e.g. Lecture week 11 · Sept 2026 Semester or a public holiday name.
          • "(overridden)" means you changed it by hand on the left.

          5b Automatic forecast & calendar analysis

          What it tells you

          The system's own reading of the forecast and calendar, with no input from you: how each meal of the chosen day looks, and what is coming up in the days ahead.

          How to read it

          • Weather by meal — rain or haze hours in each service, total rain, rain chance and PM2.5, then the expected demand across the service (e.g. 45–82%) and the worst-hour risk tier. Click a row to jump the advisor to that meal.
          • What's coming up — the current calendar period, the next change (e.g. study break in 9 days), the next public holiday, rain and haze over the next 7 days, the riskiest lunch or dinner of the week, and how far ahead the forecast reaches.
          • It always uses the live data, even while a what-if override is on.

          6 Risk card (the main answer)

          What it tells you

          How likely it is that a normal-sized batch would leave food unsold: Low Medium High surplus-waste risk.

          How to read it

          • Gauge — expected customers as % of a normal weekday.
          • Model confidence — how sure the decision tree is (low near a tier boundary).
          • 🌳 / 〰️ chips — the two AI verdicts side by side: decision tree tier and fuzzy score.
          • Amber note — appears for breakfast, tea and supper, which the survey did not cover.

          7 Savings tiles

          What it tells you

          The decision in numbers.

          How to read it

          • Recommended to cook — portions for this meal, with a ±10% range.
          • Portions not wasted — usual batch minus recommended.
          • Food saved — portions × kg per portion, plus ingredient cost in RM.
          • Energy saved — cooking energy plus warmers switched off in quiet hours, and the CO₂ this avoids.

          The per-portion figures are editable assumptions on the Model & method tab.

          8 Fuzzy logic view

          What it tells you

          How the second AI technique (a Mamdani fuzzy system) reasons with the real numbers, so 0.9 mm and 1.1 mm of rain give similar answers instead of a sudden jump.

          How to read it

          • Three input charts — the white line is today's value; the numbers below are how much it belongs to each group (e.g. rain Light 0.60 · Heavy 0.40).
          • Rules firing now — each IF–THEN rule and its strength (0–1).
          • Waste-risk chart — the combined shape; the coloured line (centroid) is the score out of 100.
          • Batch size from — choose fuzzy logic (smooth) or the survey formula (step-like).

          9 Cooking & energy plan

          What it tells you

          What to actually do, in three timed steps for the selected meal.

          How to read it

          • Low — cook the full batch, normal warmer schedule.
          • Medium — cook ~80% first, top up only if it sells.
          • High — cook ~60% first, small top-ups, switch off spare warmers and turn the hood down.
          • The note underneath warns about rain inside this meal period (e.g. "Rain from 1–3 PM: hold the next top-up").

          10 Why the model says this

          What it tells you

          The reasoning, so the vendor can trust (or question) the advice.

          How to read it

          • Survey factors — each condition's effect from the survey, e.g. Heavy rain ×0.45 means 45% of normal customers.
          • Decision-tree path — the yes/no questions the trained tree asked, ending in the risk tier and how many training days support it.

          11 Next 7 days

          What it tells you

          A week-ahead outlook at the same time of day, using each day's forecast and calendar — useful for ordering ingredients.

          How to read it

          • % = expected customers; badge and coloured bottom line = risk tier. Click a day to plan it.

          12 Expected customers by time slot

          What it tells you

          When the customers arrive within lunch (from survey Q5), normal day vs this service.

          How to read it

          • Grey bars = normal day, coloured bars = this service; the highlighted bar is your selected time. 12–1 PM is the main rush.

          Glossary

          Surplus-waste risk
          How much of a normal batch would likely go unsold. Low ≥ 80% of normal customers, Medium 55–80%, High < 55%.
          Expected demand
          Customers expected, as a % of a normal weekday.
          Multiplier
          A survey-based effect, e.g. heavy rain ×0.45 = 45% of normal customers.
          Usual batch
          What the vendor normally cooks on a normal weekday.
          Decision tree
          A flowchart of yes/no questions learned from 3,780 practice days (machine learning).
          Leaf
          The end of a decision-tree path, where the answer is given.
          Gini impurity
          How mixed a group of training days is (0 = all the same tier). The tree picks questions that lower it most.
          Confidence
          The share of similar training days that had the predicted tier.
          Fuzzy set / membership
          A group with soft edges, e.g. 1.4 mm of rain is 60% "light" and 40% "heavy".
          Rule strength
          How much an IF–THEN rule applies right now (the smallest of its memberships).
          Centroid
          The balance point of the combined fuzzy shape — the final fuzzy score.
          Live vs manual
          Live = values from the forecast and calendar; manual = values you set for "what-if" testing.
          Simulated data
          The training days were generated from survey answers because no real sales records were available.
          PM2.5
          Fine particles in the air (µg/m³). 35 or more is treated as haze.

          2-minute demo script

          1. Problem (Daily advisor): "Vendors cook the same big batch every day; when students don't come, food and gas are wasted."
          2. Live inputs: point at the week strip (today's lecture week) and the weather card (live forecast).
          3. Press ▶ Auto-play the day: dials turn with the hourly rain, the pot level drops in the storm, numbers roll.
          4. Risk card + plan: "High risk at 1 PM → cook 54 instead of 120, saving ~23 kg of food and ~29 kWh."
          5. Model & method: 93.8% accuracy, feature importance, crisp-vs-fuzzy chart.
          6. Engine console: press ▶ Replay training and show it reach 93.8% live.
          7. Limitations: survey-based simulated data; next step is real sales logs from vendors.
